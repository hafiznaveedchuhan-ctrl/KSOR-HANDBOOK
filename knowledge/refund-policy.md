A commission you've already seen appear in your Associates dashboard isn't
necessarily money you'll actually get paid — if the customer returns the
item, Amazon reverses the commission along with the sale. Understanding
when and why that happens matters as much as understanding how you earned
the commission in the first place.

## How Returns Affect Your Commission

A commission is only earned on a **qualifying purchase** — a completed,
kept sale. If a customer returns the product within Amazon's return
window, the sale is unwound, and the commission tied to it is reversed
from your account the same way the sale itself is reversed on Amazon's
side. This is not a penalty against the affiliate — it's the same logic as
any commission being tied to a completed transaction, not merely an
initiated one.[^amazon-associates-operating-agreement]

## The Commission Holding Period

Because returns can happen well after a purchase, Amazon Associates does
not pay out a commission the moment it appears. Commissions are held for a
period — long enough to cover the ordinary return window — before being
finalized and released for payment. This is why a dashboard showing strong
recent earnings doesn't mean that exact figure is what eventually gets
paid: some portion is still provisional until the holding period passes.

## Return Windows

Amazon's standard return window is commonly cited as 30 days from
delivery, but this varies by product category, seller, and season (for
example, holiday-period purchases often get an extended window). Rather
than asserting exact day-counts by category here — a figure Amazon changes
without notice — treat 30 days as the general baseline and verify the
specific category's current window on Amazon's own returns policy page
before relying on an exact number for anything time-sensitive.

## What This Means for Affiliates

- **Don't treat a fresh commission as final money.** Budget and plan
  around the holding period, not around the day a sale first appears.
- **High-return categories carry more volatility.** Electronics, apparel
  (sizing-driven returns), and gift-heavy shopping seasons tend to see
  higher return rates — expect a larger gap between what first appears and
  what's ultimately finalized when you promote heavily in these
  categories.
- **A pattern of returns on a specific product is a signal.** If a product
  you promote is generating unusually frequent returns, that's worth
  treating as a red flag about the product itself, not just a payout
  inconvenience — see `product-hunting-guide.md`'s evaluation criteria.
